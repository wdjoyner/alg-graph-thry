# Which symmetric groups have a complement?

Based on David Joyner’s Draft 6. Each heading starts an eighteen-second beat.

### 01 · Edge positions
A Rubik's Cube has twelve edge pieces. Label their positions and a rearrangement gives a permutation of twelve letters. Herbert Kociemba asked David Joyner a storage question. Could every edge permutation be specified by two subgroup coordinates, with one coordinate coming from the symmetric group on eight letters?

### 02 · Exact factorization
An exact factorization means that every element is a product of one element from H and one from K, uniquely in that order. If two products agree, rearranging puts the difference of the H coordinates in both subgroups. A trivial intersection forces that difference to be the identity.

### 03 · Six permutations, two coordinates
In the symmetric group on three letters, take H generated by the swap of one and two. Take K generated by the three-cycle. Two choices in H and three in K give six distinct products. Each cell of the grid identifies one permutation, and the row and column recover its coordinates.

### 04 · Apply the right factor first
Find the swap of one and three in the grid. First apply the inverse three-cycle: one goes to three, three to two, and two to one. Then swap the labels one and two. The composite swaps one and three and fixes two. This is the paper's rightmost-first convention.

### 05 · A stabilizer of an ordered tuple
In the standard embedding, the symmetric group on m letters permutes the first m letters and fixes the last k, where k equals n minus m. List those last letters in order. The entire stabilizer of this tuple is exactly the standard subgroup.

### 06 · One element for every target
Write a permutation as a complement element followed on the right by a stabilizer element. The right factor fixes the base tuple, so the left factor alone determines its image. If two complement elements send the base tuple to the same target, their quotient lies in the intersection, and they agree.

### 07 · The affine example
For five letters, label the fixed pair zero and one in the field of five elements. An affine map sends zero to b and one to a plus b. So for target three, two, choose b equal to three and a equal to four, doing arithmetic modulo five.

### 08 · Sharp transitivity
For any distinct target entries, their difference gives a nonzero slope. The same reasoning works from any starting pair, or we can go back through the base pair. This is sharp two-transitivity. Conversely, a unique mover to the target leaves a residual permutation fixing the base. So regularity gives a complement.

### 09 · The two universal families
For degrees above twelve there are four families. A full cycle handles a single fixed letter. At the other extreme, fixing all but two images leaves two completions of opposite parity. Exactly one is even. This makes the alternating group a complement to the standard symmetric group on two letters.

### 10 · Two field families
Two fixed letters require a prime-power degree, and the affine group supplies a complement. Three require the degree minus one to be a prime power. The projective linear group acts on that field with a point at infinity, sending any ordered triple to any other in exactly one way.

### 11 · Mathieu exceptions
Between sharp four-transitivity and the alternating case, the extra groups are Mathieu eleven in degree eleven, and Mathieu twelve in degree twelve, sharp on four and five letters respectively. The degree restrictions come from classical results of Zassenhaus and Jordan, as cited through Cameron and Dixon and Mortimer in the draft.

### 12 · Why the cube pair fails
For twelve letters, the allowed values of k are one, three, five, and ten. Eight movable letters would leave four fixed letters. Sharp four-transitivity in this range requires degree eleven. The generator scripts check specific positive examples by orders and orbits. The impossibility in degree twelve follows from classification.

### 13 · Three subgroup coordinates
The cube question has a three-coordinate answer. Use the projective line over the field of eleven elements. The group P is sharply transitive on ordered triples. The cyclic group C fixes zero, one, and infinity and cycles the remaining nine points. The final subgroup also fixes two.

### 14 · Solve for the first two factors
Take g to be the swap of zero and one. Its first three images determine u, the map one minus x. To find the C coordinate, pull the fourth target, two, back through u. That gives ten, so use the eighth power of the nine-cycle.

### 15 · Play the product rightmost first
The residual factor h fixes the base quadruple and swaps the displayed four pairs. Watch the images of the base quadruple: h fixes it, the cycle power moves two to ten, and u swaps zero and one and sends ten back to two. On the remaining points the changes cancel.

### 16 · Why every triple is unique
For any g, its first three images force u. Its fourth image then forces the cycle power, since the cycle acts regularly on nine points. The residual h is forced by multiplication with the inverse. So the three indices have sizes thirteen twenty, nine, and forty thousand three hundred twenty.

### 17 · Change one coordinate
Now use the ordered pairs of distinct field elements as vertices. Join two when exactly one coordinate changes. All pairs in a common row or column are adjacent in this layout. The base zero, one has six neighbors, three for each coordinate. This is the arrangement graph T of five, two.

### 18 · Relabel in place
Each pair b, a plus b has a unique affine label, the map a x plus b. Keep the vertices in place and follow just the six neighbors of the identity. The blue edges lead to maps fixing zero. The gold edges lead to maps fixing one. Together they give the connection set.

### 19 · Move the whole neighborhood
Apply the translation x plus one to the base and its six neighbors. Translation preserves agreement in each coordinate, so it carries those edges to the edges at one, two. In group labels we moved from the identity and d to x and x d, using left multiplication.

### 20 · Why it is Cayley
Every vertex has exactly one group label. At the vertex labeled x, all neighbors therefore have labels x d, where d runs through the six displayed maps. This is the Cayley graph rule. The script checks the edge sets for this example; the argument works for any regular action by graph automorphisms.

### 21 · A graph of sets
In T of four, two, the two orders of each pair have the same color. Forgetting order sends these two vertices to a single two-element set. The resulting Johnson graph has six vertices. Its edges join sets that share one element, or equivalently, differ by one exchange.

### 22 · Lift an edge
Look at the edge from the set one, two to the set one, three. In either ordering upstairs, replace the two by three in its own slot. The same rule gives one lift at each ordered vertex for every incident edge downstairs. The diagram highlights the two lifts.

### 23 · Degree of the covering
Now merge the two orders in each fiber. The paired edges trace the same Johnson edge. In general, a k-element set has k factorial orders, and replacing the changing symbol in its own slot gives the unique lift at every vertex. This is a k factorial fold graph covering.

### 24 · Cayley beyond complements
A complement always gives a Cayley representation. But arrangement graphs also admit coordinate permutations. Yin, Feng, Zhou, and Guo's classification, as quoted in Draft Six, gives six extra Cayley cases. Their regular groups may use coordinate permutations even when the natural symmetric group on the symbols contains no complement.
